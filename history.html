04 — History

90 Years of
Smart Metal.

Shape Memory Alloys were not invented — they were discovered, puzzled over, and slowly understood across nine decades. From a Swedish lab in 1932 to a $17 billion global industry, this is the story of one of materials science's most surprising phenomena.

Era I — Early Discoveries · 1932–1959

1932

Arne Ölander · Uppsala University, Sweden

The First Observation

Swedish physicist Arne Ölander was studying the electrochemical properties of gold-cadmium (AuCd) alloys when he noticed something strange — the alloy exhibited a "rubber-like" elastic behaviour after certain thermal treatments. He called it "pseudoelasticity" and noted it in passing, not realising the significance. This is the first documented observation of superelasticity in a metallic alloy.

Discovery

1938

Greninger & Mooradian · Harvard University, USA

Martensite Transformation Observed

Greninger and Mooradian documented the martensitic phase transformation in brass (Cu-Zn) alloys — observing that a shear-type crystal transformation occurred on cooling and reversed on heating. This was the first systematic study of the crystallographic mechanism that underlies shape memory, though its connection to the effect was not yet established.

Discovery

1951

Chang & Read · Columbia University, USA

Shape Recovery First Demonstrated

Chang and Read were the first to explicitly demonstrate and measure shape recovery in an AuCd alloy — bending a specimen and watching it return to its original shape upon heating. They measured the transformation temperatures precisely and noted the reversible nature of the effect. This is the first intentional demonstration of the shape memory effect, though the term did not yet exist.

Discovery
Era II — The Birth of Nitinol · 1960–1974

1962

William J. Buehler & Frederick Wang · US Naval Research Laboratory

Nitinol is Discovered

Naval engineer William Buehler was testing nickel-titanium alloys for missile nose cones — seeking a fatigue-resistant, heat-tolerant material. During a meeting, a folded strip of NiTi alloy was passed around the room. Someone bent it back and forth; someone else held it near a flame — and it snapped back to its original shape. The room went silent. Buehler and Frederick Wang investigated systematically and confirmed the shape memory effect. They named it Nitinol — Nickel Titanium Naval Ordnance Laboratory.

Landmark

1965

Buehler, Wang & colleagues · NRL

Superelasticity Characterised

The NRL team published comprehensive mechanical testing of Nitinol, documenting superelasticity — the ability to recover up to 8% strain upon unloading, far exceeding any conventional metal. They mapped transformation temperatures and established the Ni:Ti ratio as the primary composition variable for tuning behaviour.

Research

1969

Raychem Corporation · USA

First Commercial Application — Cryofit™

Raychem Corporation developed the first commercial SMA product: Cryofit™ pipe couplings for the US Navy's F-14 Tomcat hydraulic systems. The couplings were chilled in liquid nitrogen to expand them, slid over pipe ends, then warmed to ambient temperature — contracting to form a leakproof joint stronger than welding. Over 100,000 were installed without a single failure reported in service.

First Commercial Use

1971

Wayman & Shimizu · University of Illinois & Tohoku University

Two-Way Memory Effect Explained

Wayman and Shimizu provided the first rigorous crystallographic explanation for the two-way shape memory effect — where an alloy remembers two shapes (one for heating, one for cooling). They linked the behaviour to thermally induced martensite and internal stress fields left by training cycles, giving engineers a theoretical basis for device design.

Theory
Era III — Medical Revolution · 1975–1999

1977

Johnson & Martynova · various institutions

Orthodontic Archwires Introduced

The first Nitinol orthodontic archwires were introduced commercially. Their ability to exert constant, light force over large deformations — unlike stainless steel wires that quickly lose tension — revolutionised orthodontic treatment. Within a decade they became the industry standard for initial alignment stages, reducing patient discomfort and appointment frequency dramatically.

Medical

1983

Dotter, Cragg, Castañeda-Zúñiga · USA

First SMA Vascular Stent Concept

Interventional radiologist Charles Dotter first proposed using a self-expanding Nitinol mesh to hold open blood vessels — the concept of the modern stent. His team demonstrated the idea in animal models: a collapsed NiTi tube delivered by catheter that expanded at body temperature to scaffold the vessel. The era of interventional cardiology was quietly beginning.

Medical Landmark

1990

Various manufacturers · USA & Europe

Nitinol Eyeglass Frames

Superelastic Nitinol eyeglass frames reach the consumer market. Able to be fully deformed and spring back perfectly, they became a commercial proof-of-concept that SMA properties could survive high-volume manufacturing and everyday use — building public familiarity with "memory metal."

Consumer

1994

FDA · USA

FDA Approval — Nitinol Stents

The US Food and Drug Administration approved the first Nitinol self-expanding stent for clinical use in peripheral vascular disease. This regulatory milestone opened the floodgates: within five years, Nitinol stents became the global standard for carotid artery, iliac artery, and femoropopliteal interventions. The medical device industry's largest SMA market was born.

Regulatory Landmark
Era IV — Modern Era · 2000–Present

2003

Boeing / NASA DARPA

Morphing Aircraft Wing Research

NASA and Boeing began joint research programs embedding SMA actuators in wing structures to achieve in-flight shape change. The DARPA Smart Wing program demonstrated that Nitinol tendons could alter wing camber and twist without conventional hydraulic or mechanical actuators, reducing weight and drag — a preview of morphing aircraft now being commercialised on Boeing 737 engine chevrons.

Aerospace

2012

Trignano, Italy · Post-earthquake retrofit

SMA Seismic Retrofit — First in Europe

Following the 2012 Emilia earthquake, the historic church of San Giorgio in Trignano was retrofitted with SMA tendons — the first large-scale architectural application of SMAs for seismic protection in Europe. The system allowed the masonry to flex during aftershocks while the SMA elements returned it to vertical alignment, preserving the 18th-century structure without invasive steel reinforcement.

Civil Engineering

2022

Apple Inc. · iPhone 14 Pro

SMA Enters the Smartphone

Apple replaced the voice coil motor in the iPhone 14 Pro's Optical Image Stabilisation system with a four-wire SMA actuator array — making the iPhone the first mass-market consumer product to use SMA actuation at scale. With over 200 million iPhones sold per year, this single design decision brought SMA technology to more people than all previous applications combined.

Consumer Landmark

2024–

Global research groups & industry

4D Printing & Programmable Matter

The frontier of SMA research now involves 4D printing — using additive manufacturing to create objects with programmed shape changes built into their geometry. Combined with machine learning optimisation of alloy compositions and training protocols, researchers are designing SMA systems that respond to multiple stimuli (heat, stress, magnetic field) with precisely engineered deformation sequences.

💡

The Accidental Discovery Pattern: Like many great materials discoveries, Nitinol was found by people looking for something else entirely. Buehler was seeking fatigue-resistant missile nose cone material. The shape memory effect revealed itself at a lab meeting — not a planned experiment. This underscores why broad, exploratory materials research funding remains essential: the next revolutionary material may already be sitting on a bench, waiting for someone to notice.
